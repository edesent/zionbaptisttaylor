*Ephesians 1:15–23*

Every generation of believers lives under powers that look bigger than the church. In Ephesus, those powers wore a goddess's robes and filled the city's bookshelves with spells.

When the gospel took hold there, new converts burned their books of magic in public, and the books were worth "fifty thousand pieces of silver" (Acts 19:19). Not long afterward, a silversmith named Demetrius stirred up a riot because the gospel was hurting the trade in silver shrines. For about two hours the crowd shouted, "Great is Diana of the Ephesians!" (Acts 19:34).

Paul writes to that church: small, surrounded, and outnumbered. Yet he doesn't ask God to remove the powers. He asks God to open their eyes to the One seated far above them.

That's why this passage fits Christ the King Sunday, the last Lord's Day before Advent. Before we look ahead to the King's coming, we look up to the King who already reigns. In Greek, Paul's prayer runs as one long sentence. It climbs from thanksgiving, through three things God wants us to know, all the way to a throne.

## Pray for Eyes to See the King

Christ's reign is a fact, but we need God to give us eyes to see it.

Paul begins with thanks. He has heard of the Ephesians' "faith in the Lord Jesus and your love for all the saints," and so he says, "[I] do not cease to give thanks for you" (vv. 15–16). Faith looks up to Christ, and love reaches out to His people. Paul names the same pair in Colossians 1:4 and Philemon 5. That's how he measured a healthy church.

Paul thanks God before he asks God for anything, and we should start where he started. When you pray for our church, thank God for every believer whose faith rests on Christ and whose love reaches the saints. You aren't alone. Christ has others who bear His name and share the load.

Then Paul asks. He prays that "the Father of glory, may give to you the spirit of wisdom and revelation in the knowledge of Him, the eyes of your understanding being enlightened" (vv. 17–18). He prays this for people who already believe. Even mature saints need a fresh sight of Christ.

"Being enlightened" translates a perfect participle (πεφωτισμένους): light God gave and keeps in place. Seeing the King is a gift of grace, from the first glimpse to every glimpse after.

Here's where many of us struggle. Christ's throne doesn't go vacant on Monday morning, but our eyes drift. We look at the headline, the bank statement, the test results, or the empty chair at the table, and the King drops out of view.

So pray Paul's prayer before you turn on the news, when you see the empty chair, and when you open the bank statement. Pray it for your children, for the person beside you in the pew, and for our church.

## Know What the King Has Secured for You

Paul wants us to know three things, and each is larger than the one before it.

First, "the hope of His calling" (v. 18). God called you, and He called you toward something. Biblical hope isn't a wish. It's a settled expectation, guaranteed by the Holy Spirit "until the redemption of the purchased possession" (1:14). You may feel discouraged, but your future hasn't gotten smaller. You've only lost sight of it.

Second, "the riches of the glory of His inheritance in the saints" (v. 18). Paul doesn't say our inheritance; he says His. Moses sang, "For the LORD's portion is His people; Jacob is the place of His inheritance" (Deut. 32:9), and Paul applies that ancient line to a church of Jewish and Gentile believers in Ephesus. The King who owns everything looks at His church and calls her rich, and He counts you among His riches.

Third, "the exceeding greatness of His power toward us who believe" (v. 19). Paul packs four words for power into this one verse: δύναμις, ἐνέργεια, κράτος, and ἰσχύς. The verb behind "exceeding" (ὑπερβάλλω) appears five times in the New Testament, all of them in Paul and three of them in Ephesians (2 Cor. 3:10; 9:14; Eph. 1:19; 2:7; 3:19).

Feeling weak doesn't mean you are weak. Your strength doesn't rise and fall with your emotions, your health, or your bank account. The Father's power works toward you, and Paul is about to show how much of it there is.

## Behold the King Enthroned

The Father measures His power toward us by what He has already done for His Son. It is the power "which He worked in Christ" (v. 20), and Paul names four acts. The Father raised Him, seated Him, put all things under His feet, and gave Him to the church.

Death is the one enemy no human king has ever defeated, from Pharaoh, to Caesar, to every ruler since. Yet God raised Jesus, "because it was not possible that He should be held by it" (Acts 2:24). That's the power at work toward you.

The Father then "seated Him at His right hand in the heavenly places" (v. 20), fulfilling the promise of Psalm 110:1: "Sit at My right hand, till I make Your enemies Your footstool." A seated king has finished his campaign and now rules. Paul uses the phrase "in the heavenly places" five times in this letter (1:3, 20; 2:6; 3:10; 6:12). Christ sits there, believers are seated there with Him (2:6), and hostile powers still wage war there (6:12).

Christ sits "far above all principality and power and might and dominion, and every name that is named" (v. 21). The word for "might" (δύναμις) is the same word Paul just used for God's power toward us in verse 19. Every might in the universe sits beneath the might that works for you.

So name the powers that frighten you: the market, the cancer, the cultural tide, the accuser of the brethren. Christ sits above every one of them, "not only in this age but also in that which is to come." Peter tied God's oath to seat David's Son on his throne to Jesus' exaltation at the Father's right hand (Acts 2:30–33). Jesus reigns now, and when He returns He will rule openly over the earth He redeemed.

The Father also "put all things under His feet" (v. 22), echoing Psalm 8:6. Adam lost dominion in the garden; Christ, the last Adam, has received it. "But now we do not yet see all things put under him. But we see Jesus... crowned with glory and honor" (Heb. 2:8–9).

Abraham Kuyper said it memorably in 1880, at the opening of the Free University of Amsterdam: "There is not a square inch in the whole domain of our human existence over which Christ, who is sovereign over all, does not cry: 'Mine!'" No square inch of your life lies outside His claim, from your work, to your marriage, to your money. Don't keep a room where the King isn't welcome.

## Rejoice in the King Given to the Church

The Father gave the One who rules everything to the church as her Head. "And gave Him to be head over all things to the church" (v. 22). The verb is the language of gift. Everything Christ rules, He rules for His people.

Our confession puts it this way: "The Lord Jesus Christ is the Head of the church, in whom, by the appointment of the Father, all power for the calling, institution, order or government of the church, is invested in a supreme and sovereign manner" (2LCF 26.4). No pastor, elder, board, or culture holds that crown. That humbles us, because we don't run this church. It also steadies us, because the King who rules the nations rules for us.

Paul calls the church "His body, the fullness of Him who fills all in all" (v. 23). He prays that we "may be filled with all the fullness of God" (3:19), and he says Christ ascended "that He might fill all things" (4:10). We aren't a club or a weekly event. We're the place on earth where the ascended King's fullness dwells.

Polycarp, the aged pastor of Smyrna, knew that King. When the authorities arrested him around AD 155, the police captain Herod and his father Nicetes took him into their carriage and pressed him: "Why what harm is there in saying, Caesar is Lord, and offering incense...?" He refused (Mart. Pol. 8.2).

In the arena, the proconsul promised to release him if he would revile Christ. Polycarp answered, "Fourscore and six years have I been His servant, and He hath done me no wrong. How then can I blaspheme my King who saved me?" (Mart. Pol. 9.3). He called Jesus his King in front of Caesar's representative, because he knew the One beneath whose feet every power lies, the proconsul's included.

Most of us will never stand before a proconsul. But every day we face rival lords: the approval of others, the comfort of money, the fear of the future. Each of them asks for a little incense. Refuse them, and confess Christ as King at home, at work, and in your private thoughts.

## Lift Your Eyes to the Throne

Paul prayed that we would see our hope, God's inheritance, and God's power. Then he showed us how great that power is: a risen Christ, seated far above all, with all things under His feet, given to the church as her Head.

The King isn't waiting to take His throne. He sits on it now, and one day heaven will announce, "The kingdoms of this world have become the kingdoms of our Lord and of His Christ, and He shall reign forever and ever!" (Rev. 11:15).

So lift your eyes, church. Your enemies are under His feet, His power works toward you, and your Head sits far above all.
