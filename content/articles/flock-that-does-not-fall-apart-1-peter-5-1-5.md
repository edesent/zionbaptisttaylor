*1 Peter 5:1–5*

In 1915, Antarctic pack ice crushed Ernest Shackleton’s ship, the Endurance. Her twenty-eight men were left camped on drifting ice, cold and hungry, with no hope of rescue anytime soon. Hunger and fear can tear any company apart, and nothing guaranteed these men would hold together.

Shackleton had only eighteen fur sleeping bags for twenty-eight men. The other ten would get lighter, colder wool bags. He decided to hand out the fur bags by lot, but, as he wrote afterward, “some of us older hands did not join in the lottery.” The leaders took the cold bags so that the men would have the warm ones.

All twenty-eight came home alive. Leaders who serve rather than help themselves are how a company holds together under pressure, and Peter says the same thing to the church.

## Elders Who Shepherd Willingly and by Example

Peter begins with the church’s leaders: “The elders who are among you I exhort, I who am a fellow elder” (5:1). He could have pulled rank as an apostle. Instead he calls himself a “fellow elder” (συμπρεσβύτερος), a word found nowhere else in the New Testament and quite possibly his own coinage. He speaks from beside the elders, not over them.

Peter’s “therefore” (οὖν) ties this charge to the suffering he described in chapter 4. A church under pressure from outside can’t afford disorder inside, and the flock belongs to God: “Shepherd the flock of God which is among you” (5:2). God has always taken it personally when shepherds feed themselves instead of the sheep (Ezek. 34:2).

Peter frames the work with three contrasts. Elders serve “not by compulsion but willingly, not for dishonest gain but eagerly; nor as being lords over those entrusted to you, but being examples to the flock” (5:2–3). Jesus drew the same line between rulers who lord it over people and servants who give themselves away (Mark 10:42–45).

All three contrasts rest on a promise: “and when the Chief Shepherd appears, you will receive the crown of glory that does not fade away” (5:4). “Chief Shepherd” (ἀρχιποίμην) appears only here in the New Testament. An elder who serves toward His return doesn’t need recognition now.

Eugene Peterson lived that way. In the early 1960s he was an associate pastor at a Presbyterian church in White Plains, New York, planning to finish a Ph.D. in Semitic studies. Then, in 1962, he was asked to plant a congregation in Bel Air, Maryland, outside Baltimore. He set the academic path aside and stayed twenty-nine years.

For most of those years he was unknown beyond that congregation in what he called “soggy suburbia,” and nearly two decades passed before his books began to reach a wider audience. He admired the Swiss physician Paul Tournier for the “no slippage between what he was saying and the way he was living,” and he sought that same integrity in a quiet pulpit. No one needed to see it for him to do it.

If you lead anyone in our church as an elder, a deacon, or a teacher, Peter’s contrasts give you a test for this week. Am I serving because I have to, because of what it gets me, or because I want to be the one who decides? Find one place where you can lead by example instead of instruction, and do the thing you’re asking others to do.

If you don’t lead, pray for our elders by name. The pressure on a church often lands on them first.

## Younger Members Who Submit to Their Leaders

“Likewise you younger people, submit yourselves to your elders” (5:5). The word “likewise” ties this to what came before. Leaders serve in one way, and those they lead respond in another. Peter most likely means those who are literally younger, who often resist authority more readily.

Submission here isn’t blind obedience. No member should follow a leader who contradicts God’s Word or the gospel, and elders remain accountable to the congregation. What Peter rules out is resisting every initiative and complaining about every direction.

Francis Asbury knew both sides of this. In 1771, when John Wesley asked a conference in Bristol who would go to America, Asbury volunteered. He was a Birmingham-area man whose schooling had ended in boyhood, and he was twenty-six when he landed.

Over the following decades he sent young preachers across circuits that could stretch hundreds of miles. They didn’t choose their appointments, and they were paid very little. Yet the movement grew from 42 preachers in 1780 to 227 in 1790.

Submission ran the other direction too. When Thomas Coke arrived in 1784 to set Asbury apart as superintendent, Asbury refused to accept the office on Wesley’s word alone and insisted that the preachers elect him at the Christmas Conference in Baltimore. The young followed, and the leader stayed accountable.

Our culture teaches us to submit to no one and keep every option open. Peter addresses the younger, but the principle reaches everyone who sits under leadership, and it starts with where you take your concerns. If you have a question about our church’s direction, take it to an elder face to face, not to the parking lot or the group text. Then follow what’s decided, as long as it doesn’t ask you to disobey God.

A church holds together when its members would rather be led than proven right.

## Everyone Clothed With Humility

Then Peter widens the circle to everyone: “Yes, all of you be submissive to one another, and be clothed with humility” (5:5). The verb (ἐγκομβώσασθε) pictures tying on a garment, perhaps a servant’s apron. Humility isn’t a feeling we wait for. It’s something we put on where others can see it.

Peter gives the reason from Proverbs 3:34, as it reads in the Greek Old Testament: “God resists the proud, but gives grace to the humble” (cf. James 4:6). People who remember they are creatures and sinners take offense less easily, and that keeps a church’s relationships whole.

John Wesley and George Whitefield had every reason to part ways for good. In 1739 Wesley preached and published his sermon “Free Grace,” attacking the doctrine of election that Whitefield held. Whitefield answered in a letter dated Christmas Eve 1740 and published early the next year. The disagreement was real, and the revival movement split into two camps.

Yet within a few years the two men were reconciled, and Whitefield later helped make peace when John and Charles Wesley quarreled with each other. When Whitefield died at Newburyport, Massachusetts, on September 30, 1770, he had asked that Wesley preach his funeral sermon. On November 18 in London, Wesley did, from Numbers 23:10. They kept their convictions, and they kept their affection.

Think of the last time someone at church offended you, and ask what was underneath it. Often it’s pride: the quiet insistence that our idea should have won or our feelings should have come first. This week, go to that person. Own your part, or let the offense go and don’t bring it up again.

Then do one small, visible act of humility. Take the lesser task, or ask someone’s opinion and really listen. A church that wears humility is a church God is already helping.

## Holding Together Under the Chief Shepherd

Peter gives us one picture: a flock held together by shepherds who lead willingly and a people who follow humbly, all of them under the Chief Shepherd who is coming back.

So pray for your pastor and elders by name every day. Take your concerns to an elder face to face, and then follow what’s decided. And go to the one person here you’ve had a dispute with, own your part, and let the rest go.

“God resists the proud, but gives grace to the humble.” Put on humility this week, and the flock will hold together.
