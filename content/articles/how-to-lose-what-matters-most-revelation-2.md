*Revelation 2:1–7*

Roughly one in four American divorces now involves a couple over fifty. Researchers call it “gray divorce,” and the Pew Research Center reports that the divorce rate for that age group has roughly doubled since 1990.

Most of these couples never fought in public. They kept the same house, the same routines, and the same anniversary dinners for decades. What died, years before anyone filed paperwork, wasn’t the form of the marriage. It was the love that gave the form its reason for existing.

Christ says something much like this to the church at Ephesus. A congregation can keep every right practice in place and still lose the one thing that made any of it matter: “Nevertheless I have this against you, that you have left your first love” (Rev. 2:4). How does a church doing everything right lose what matters most?

## Orthodoxy Can Mask a Loss of Devotion

Christ opens with “I know your works” (v. 2), and what follows describes a church running at full strength. The Ephesians labored, endured, tested false apostles and found them liars, and hated the deeds of the Nicolaitans, “which I also hate” (v. 6).

None of this had stopped. Christ speaks of their stand against evil in the present tense: “you cannot bear those who are evil,” and “you hate the deeds of the Nicolaitans.” That’s what makes verse 4 so unsettling. The loss didn’t show up as a decline in any of these practices; it hid underneath them.

The churches of New England had walked this road by 1679. When the Reforming Synod met in Boston that year, those congregations had kept their confession, their discipline, and their worship intact for two generations. Their doctrine hadn’t changed since the founders.

Yet the synod’s report, drafted by Increase Mather and published as *The Necessity of Reformation*, catalogued a long list of “provoking evils” in churches that remained orthodox on paper: decay in godliness, coldness in prayer, and fading zeal for the very truths they still confessed correctly. They weren’t failing an examination of their creed. They were failing a test their creed could not detect.

So we can’t treat attendance, giving, or doctrinal soundness as proof that our love for Christ remains strong. Ephesus had all three and still heard this rebuke.

Pick one place where your part of our church’s life has stayed perfectly consistent: a class still taught, a ministry still running, a doctrine still defended. Ask honestly whether love for Christ still animates it, or whether habit alone carries it forward. A practice can outlive the love that started it by years.

I’ve asked this question prayerfully about our church. I want to be sure we aren’t continuing out of habit, but out of earnest love for Christ and a desire to see others brought to Him and grown up in Him as disciples.

## The Loss Comes Before We Notice It

Verse 4 changes the grammar. The commendations of verses 2–3 describe a church at work, but the charge comes in the aorist, ἀφῆκες, “you have left.” Christ doesn’t say the Ephesians are drifting from their first love; He says they have left it.

He isn’t reporting a trend He’s watching develop. He’s announcing a fact to a congregation that, by its own measure, had noticed nothing wrong.

Sherron Watkins saw something similar at Enron. The company reported record profits and a rising stock price and was celebrated as one of America’s most admired corporations. On August 15, 2001, Watkins sent chairman Kenneth Lay a memo warning that the company might “implode in a wave of accounting scandals.”

When Enron filed for bankruptcy that December, the shock wasn’t only that it failed. It was that so few realized it had failed internally long before.

Don’t assume that if something this serious had happened to you, you’d already know. The Ephesians didn’t know, and not because they were careless. A completed loss doesn’t announce itself the way a declining one does.

Ask someone who knows you well (a spouse, an elder, a close friend in the faith) whether they see in you the same love for Christ they saw a year ago. Ask them to answer honestly rather than kindly. You may need someone outside your own assessment to tell you what Christ already knows.

Here’s one test for a whole church: listen to what we talk about, what we pray for, and what we give to. If those show more affection for the church’s survival and comfort than for Christ Himself, the love of verse 4 may already be gone.

A church our age, with a pastor who has served as long as I have, faces a particular danger. We leave our first love when “that’s the way we’ve always done it” settles every question, or when we long to return to how things were more than we long to obey Christ now, in this culture, at this time. Tradition is good, but it becomes traditionalism when it stops serving the gospel and starts judging every proposal.

## Remember, Repent, Return

Christ answers His diagnosis with three commands: “Remember therefore from where you have fallen; repent and do the first works” (v. 5). Remembering looks back to recover what was lost. Repenting turns from the course that caused the loss, and doing the first works puts that repentance into the concrete practices that marked the earlier love.

Then comes the warning: “or else I will come to you quickly and remove your lampstand from its place, unless you repent” (v. 5). The stakes aren’t merely diminished affection. They’re the church’s existence as a light-bearing congregation.

And a church can lose its lampstand and keep functioning, even appear to thrive. The Western Roman Empire kept its emperors and its Senate for generations after real power had passed elsewhere. The forms outlived the substance.

There’s a hopeful side to this, too. On September 23, 1857, Jeremiah Lanphier, a lay city missionary in New York, opened a noon prayer meeting for businessmen in Lower Manhattan. Six men came. Within weeks a financial panic swept the city, and within months thousands were gathering daily across New York to pray.

No new doctrine drove what followed. The revival spread to other cities and is credited with bringing hundreds of thousands into the churches within two years, because believers did what Christ commands in verse 5: they remembered, they repented, and they did the first works again.

Don’t treat these commands as sentiment attached to a mostly healthy church. Write down what your love for Christ looked like when it was freshest: the prayers you prayed, the urgency you felt, the people you told about Him.

Then name, specifically, what has replaced that urgency, and repent of it. Return to one concrete practice from that earlier season this week, not someday. A removed lampstand doesn’t relight itself.

## Keep the Lamp Lit

Christ’s warning to Ephesus was never only about Ephesus. It speaks to any church, this one included, that mistakes faithful practice for undiminished love. Continuity doesn’t prove devotion.

So let’s give ourselves to prayer that asks nothing of God except that He rekindle our love for Christ Himself. Let’s examine whether that love still lives in each of us, and in all of us together. And as we keep reading Scripture together, singing, and receiving the preached Word, let’s make sure love for Christ is what moves us to do it.

Remember. Repent. Return. The lampstand is still here. Let’s keep it lit.
