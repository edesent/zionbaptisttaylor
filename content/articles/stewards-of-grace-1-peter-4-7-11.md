*1 Peter 4:7–11*

When the Roman armies closed around Jerusalem in AD 70, many in the city refused to believe the siege would succeed. Josephus records that false prophets kept promising divine deliverance right up to the end, and people listened. They kept up their ordinary routines, their commerce, and their quarrels while the city collapsed around them.

The church in Jerusalem had already gone. According to Eusebius, the believers there left for Pella, across the Jordan, before the war reached the city, and many since have connected their flight with Jesus' warning to "flee to the mountains" (Matt. 24:16). They survived because they took the warning seriously while there was still time to act.

Peter makes the same kind of argument to believers scattered across Asia Minor: "But the end of all things is at hand" (1 Pet. 4:7). A church that believes it will live differently from one that doesn't. So how should we serve one another while there's still time?

## Pray With a Clear Head

Peter draws a direct line from the nearness of the end to prayer: "therefore be serious and watchful in your prayers" (4:7). He doesn't say, "The end is near, so panic," or "The end is near, so withdraw." He says the end is near, so pray.

Prayer requires a clear mind. The two verbs Peter uses (σωφρονήσατε καὶ νήψατε) mean to be sound-minded and sober, the opposite of the drunkenness and excess he listed just a few verses earlier (4:3). A church that has broken with Babylon's pattern can now pray with the clarity the hour demands.

Martin Luther understood how easily that clarity slips away. In 1535 his barber, Peter Beskendorf, asked him how to pray, and Luther answered with a short book. He advised making prayer the first business of the morning and the last of the evening, and he warned against the voice that says, "Wait a little; I'll pray in an hour, once I've finished this." Before long, he said, the whole day is gone and prayer with it.

Luther wasn't writing for monks. He was writing for a tradesman with a shop to run, because he knew that a busy life, left unguarded, crowds out prayer.

Babylon works hard to keep our minds clouded. Financial worry, social unrest, and the endless scroll of bad news all produce a scattered, reactive mind that can't hold a thought long enough to direct it toward God. This week, name what has been clouding your mind and set it aside deliberately, not only for your own peace, but because your church family needs you to pray for them.

## Love Fervently and Open Your Home

"And above all things have fervent love for one another, for 'love will cover a multitude of sins'" (4:8). The word ἐκτενής describes a love that stretches out and keeps going. It isn't the easy affection of people who happen to like each other; it's the deliberate, persevering love that holds a church together.

That kind of love covers sin. Peter draws the line from Proverbs 10:12 ("Hatred stirs up strife, but love covers all sins"), and the point is that love absorbs a brother's failures instead of broadcasting them. It chooses the costly path of forbearance over the easy path of exposure.

Hospitality is that same love given an address: "Be hospitable to one another without grumbling" (4:9). In a world where traveling believers depended on Christian homes, opening the door was no small courtesy.

Priscilla and Aquila show what it looks like. They appear in six New Testament texts across four books. Paul lodged and worked alongside them in Corinth (Acts 18:2–3), and a church met in their home in Ephesus (1 Cor. 16:19) and again in Rome (Rom. 16:5).

They took Apollos aside and explained the way of God to him more accurately (Acts 18:26). Paul says they "risked their own necks" for his life (Rom. 16:4). Nowhere does the record hint that they counted the cost or resented it.

Grumbling is the sound of love that has decided it has given enough. It's the sigh before you answer the door, the quiet calculation of what this will cost you. Peter expects inconvenience; what he forbids is the complaint.

So this week, think of one person whose failure you've been more inclined to mention than to cover, and cover it. Then think of one person whose need could be met by your table, your home, or your time, and open the door gladly.

## Steward the Gift God Has Given You

"As each one has received a gift, minister it to one another, as good stewards of the manifold grace of God" (4:10). The word "manifold" (ποικίλης) echoes the "various trials" of 1:6 (ποικίλοις). God's grace is as varied as the trials that test His people, and both come from the same hand.

Peter names two broad kinds of gifts: speaking and serving. "If anyone speaks, let him speak as the oracles of God. If anyone ministers, let him do it as with the ability which God supplies" (4:11). Every gift is to be exercised in God's strength, not our own, so that it never becomes a platform for self-display.

William Carey labored in Bengal for seven years before he saw a single Bengali convert. Then, in December 1800, Krishna Pal, a carpenter, was baptized. Pal brought more than his testimony; he brought his gifts.

He preached to his own people, and he wrote Bengali hymns, one of which is still sung in English translation. A mission that had seen almost no fruit began to grow, in part because one new believer put what God had given him to work for everyone else.

Stewardship cuts two ways. Your gift isn't yours to withhold, because a steward doesn't decide whether to use what was entrusted to him. And it isn't yours to take credit for, because a steward doesn't own what he manages.

Both failures are common. Some sit on their gifts waiting for a better opportunity or a more appreciative audience, and others use their gifts to draw attention to themselves. This week, use your gift in the life of the church, in the strength God supplies, and point the result away from yourself.

## Do All of It for God's Glory

Peter closes with a doxology: "that in all things God may be glorified through Jesus Christ, to whom belong the glory and the dominion forever and ever. Amen" (4:11). This isn't a pious ornament tacked onto practical advice. It's the purpose behind everything that came before.

"In all things" (ἐν πᾶσιν) sweeps back over prayer, love, hospitality, and every gift, and points them all toward one end. Babylon organizes its life around self-interest and social exchange. The church organizes its life around the glory of God, and that difference keeps us from becoming one more social club.

David Brainerd lived this way. In 1744, at twenty-six, he was preaching to Native Americans at the Forks of the Delaware in Pennsylvania, through an interpreter, with little visible fruit. Again and again his diary records grief, not that the work was hard, but that he had lived so much to himself and done so little for God.

He held every activity (preaching, visiting, praying) up to the same question: is this for His glory or mine? After Brainerd's death, Jonathan Edwards published his diary in 1749 because he believed it showed what true devotion to God looks like.

The test of 4:11 isn't only whether we do the right things, but why we do them. A person can pray, love, host, and serve in ways organized around a reputation for being a good Christian. Before you obey this week, ask who benefits most from your obedience. If the honest answer is you, the action may be right while the direction is wrong.

## Serve Now

"The end of all things is at hand." Peter says it not to frighten us, but to clarify what matters and how little time we have to do it. Your church family needs your prayers, your love, your home, and your gift.

So this week, clear your mind and pray for three people in our church by name. Cover one failure you've been tempted to mention, and open your home to someone before Sunday comes again. Use your gift now, not next month and not when you feel ready, in the strength God supplies and for His glory.

Stewards of God's grace don't wait for better conditions. They serve now.
